Richard Acquah-Sarpong, PhD

Postdoctoral Research Fellow, Western Rural Development Center, University of Idaho. PhD in Applied Economics, Oregon State University.

I study how local economies respond to policy changes and environmental shocks, with a focus on rural communities in the American West. I look at these questions through the businesses that anchor local economies: whether they survive, relocate, or stay financially sound. One line of my work evaluates place-based policies, such as Oregon's tiered minimum wage and the Kansas tax experiment. Another examines natural hazards, from destructive wildfires to wildfire smoke. I combine establishment-level microdata with quasi-experimental designs, and at the Western Rural Development Center I turn findings into reports and briefs for policymakers and communities.

Published2026 · Taxes

Do tax cuts ease liquidity constraints?

Acquah-Sarpong, R., Chen, Y., Guo, D. & Lewin, P.

International Tax and Public Finance, 2026

Journal article
AbstractThis paper examines how the Kansas tax experiment, which eliminated state income taxes on pass-through entities between 2012 and 2017, affected firms’ debt payment behavior, a key indicator of liquidity constraint. Using establishment-level data from the National Establishment Time Series (NETS) and exploiting the geographic discontinuity in the Kansas City metropolitan area, we estimate the causal effect of the reform using a spatially anchored difference-in-differences framework. The results show that eliminating pass-through income led to a measurable, but temporary, improvement in the timeliness of debt payments. The average duration of delayed payments was reduced by one-third in the baseline model. The effects were heterogeneous and significant in small and non-publicly listed establishments.

Revised & resubmittedMinimum wages

The impact of Oregon's tiered minimum wage policy on firm survival

Acquah-Sarpong, R., Chen, Y. & Lewin, P.

Annals of Regional Science, revised and resubmitted

Manuscript
AbstractIn 2016, Oregon replaced its statewide minimum wage with a geographically tiered policy that set different minimum wages in urban and rural counties and committed to a schedule of wage increases between 2016 and 2021. Using proprietary establishment-level data from the National Establishment Time Series (NETS), we evaluate the policy’s effect on establishments’ survival using a difference-in-differences discrete-time linear hazard model and cumulative survival model. Our estimates indicate a persistent increase in the annual probability of exit for incumbent establishments during the reform years (2016–2021). These repeated increases in exit risk accumulated over time, resulting in a significant, progressively larger decline in the probability of establishment survival among existing low-wage establishments during the post-reform period. We find limited evidence that the regional minimum-wage difference introduced by the policy had differential effects across urban and rural establishments. The effects are concentrated among small and financially vulnerable establishments.
Under reviewMinimum wages

Do tiered minimum wages widen regional gaps in firm performance?

Acquah-Sarpong, R., Chen, Y. & Lewin, P.

Annals of Regional Science, under review

Manuscript
AbstractThis paper studies how tiered minimum wage policies affect firms' financial health and contribute to regional divergence in firm performance. Using the introduction of a geographically tiered minimum wage structure in Oregon, we examine how spatial variation in minimum wages alters firms' ability to meet short-term financial obligations. The results reveal a significant difference in payment timeliness between establishments located inside the Portland Urban Growth Boundary (UGB), which are subject to a higher minimum wage, and those outside, which face a lower minimum wage after the policy was implemented. We find that this policy has led to a 2.1% to 3.4% difference in debt payment timeliness. Additionally, we find that the policy's impact is especially strong for establishments located near the boundary, while those farther away experience weaker effects. Although this policy was initially designed with low-wage sectors, such as food services and retail, in mind, the analysis shows that its effects also reach other industries that are typically not classified as minimum-wage sectors.

Working paperWildfires

The impact of destructive wildfires on business relocation

Acquah-Sarpong, R., Chen, Y. & Lewin, P.

Draft
AbstractDo wildfires make businesses move? We link establishment relocations to wildfire perimeters and structure losses in census tracts of eleven western states from 2010 to 2021, and compare burned tracts with never-burned tracts in the same county and fire-hazard class. Large fires that destroy few structures have no effect: moves out change by 0.01 establishments per tract-year (standard error 0.12), which rules out effects beyond 7 percent. Fires that destroyed 100 or more structures raise moves out by 1.09 per tract-year for three years (standard error 0.40), or 26 percent. The added moves stay within the county and concentrate where the fire reached establishments; we find no evidence that they go to places with lower fire hazard. Tracts hit by these fires also add seven fewer establishments a year than comparison tracts, half of that through lost entry. The primary comparison rests on 17 destructive fires and leans on the 2018 season: randomization inference gives p = 0.068 for moves, and the 2020 fires do not reproduce the pattern. We treat the null for ordinary large fires as firm and the destructive-fire effects as suggestive.

Do large tax cuts stimulate local business activity? Evidence from the Kansas tax experiment

Acquah-Sarpong, R., Agyemang-Duah, E., Chen, Y. & Lewin, P.

Draft
AbstractIn 2013 Kansas stopped taxing pass-through business income, the largest state-level experiment in taxing entrepreneurship on record. We ask whether the exemption enlarged the physical business base, using the Kansas–Missouri line that runs through the middle of the Kansas City metropolitan area. Applying a difference-in-discontinuities design to 546,562 establishment records, dated to the calendar year each describes, we find no positive effect. From the bill's signing in 2012 the establishment stock on the Kansas side fell by about 12 percent relative to the Missouri side, and every one of 26 specifications returns a negative estimate. The decline is not a shift in industry mix, is concentrated among establishments eligible for the exemption, and tends to be smaller in sectors where pass-through organization is more common. The magnitude, though not its sign, is sensitive to the arbitrary placement of the spatial grid, which we document as a general limit on grid-based border designs.
